How perfect pitch is learned

What you are training, what research says adults can reach, and how to make it a daily habit.

What you are training

Perfect pitch, or absolute pitch, is naming a note, or singing it, with no reference note to compare against. It has two halves: hearing (you hear a sound and know it is F♯) and producing (you think of F♯ and can sing it). NoteSense trains both.

The key idea is pitch class, sometimes called chroma. Every C, low or high, piano or voice, shares one quality. You are learning to recognise that quality directly, the way you recognise red without comparing it to blue.

Can adults learn it?

For decades it was thought to require training in early childhood. Newer studies are more hopeful, with a caveat: it takes many hours, and people vary.

  • In a 2019 University of Chicago study, the best adult learner passed every test of genuine absolute pitch after eight weeks of training and still had it at least four months later.
  • Across experiments by Wong and colleagues, 12 to 40 hours of gamified training taught most adults to name 7 to 9 of the 12 pitches.
  • A 2025 University of Surrey study trained 12 adult musicians online for eight weeks, about 21 hours each, and focused on pitch class rather than single piano keys, as NoteSense does.

Expect weeks to months of steady practice. Your progress chart will show you the truth.

Hearing notes in everyday life

The end goal is to hear a microwave beep or a kettle and simply know the note. Everyday life is full of steady pitches to practice on:

  • Beeps and chimes. Microwaves, dishwashers, card readers, doorbells and phones each repeat the same pitch, every time. Learn one and it becomes a personal reference note you hear daily.
  • Hums. Mains electricity hums at 60 Hz in North America, almost exactly between B♭ and B, and at 50 Hz in Europe and most of the world, a little above G. Transformers and old lights often hum one octave higher, which is the same note.
  • Between the notes. Appliances aren't tuned to music, so many sounds fall between two notes. Name the nearest one anyway: that is exactly what people with perfect pitch do.

To practice for this, switch on the everyday sounds and short notes in Settings: real-world sounds are often brief blips, not long piano tones. When something beeps, guess the note first, then check it with any free tuner app. Every sound you learn this way becomes another reference point in your day.

How NoteSense trains you

  • Few notes first, far apart. You start with C, F♯ and A. A new note unlocks when you reach 85% on your last 30 answers and every note is at 70% or more.
  • Many octaves, four sounds. Piano, pure tone, mallet and strings across three octaves, so you learn the note itself, not one recording of it.
  • Noise between notes. A short whoosh wipes the last note from your ear, so you cannot work out the answer from the interval. That would be relative pitch, a different skill.
  • Silent buttons. The app never plays musical sounds for taps or right answers, so nothing pollutes your sense of pitch.
  • Your own cues. In the Cues tab, give each note a colour, a mood and a piece of music. Notes tied to personal associations are easier to recall, and the cue shows up when you sing or imagine a note.
  • Sing it back. Producing a note from memory strengthens the same memory you use to recognise it.

Put it on your home screen

iPhone

  1. Open your NoteSense link in Safari. Tap the ⋯ button, then Share, then Add to Home Screen. Leave Open as Web App switched on and tap Add.
  2. Train from that icon from now on. A home-screen app keeps its own progress, separate from Safari.
  3. The icon doubles as your one-tap drill: open the app after 30 minutes away and it starts with a quick ping. You can turn this off in Preferences.
  4. For nudges through the day, make a repeating reminder in the Reminders app, such as NoteSense at 10:00, 14:00 and 19:00.

Android

  1. Open your NoteSense link in Chrome, tap the menu, then Add to Home screen or Install app.
  2. Long-press the icon for Quick ping and Train shortcuts. Drag Quick ping onto your home screen for a one-tap drill.
  3. For nudges through the day, set repeating reminders in Google Calendar or Google Tasks.

iPhone widget: a note to imagine

A widget can't play sound, but it can show you a note to imagine, which trains the producing half of perfect pitch. Use it at small (2×2) or medium (4×2) size. The medium size adds Pip, your cue for the note and the notes in your set. It shows a different note from your set every hour. Tap it and Safari opens a quick check that plays the note. The check needs nothing from your progress, so it works even though Safari and the app store progress separately.

  1. Install the free Scriptable app from the App Store.
  2. Paste the link the widget should open, then copy the script below. It contains your current notes, so copy it again when you unlock more.
  3. In Scriptable, tap +, paste, and name it NoteSense.
  4. Long-press your home screen, tap Edit, then Add Widget. Choose Scriptable, swipe to the medium size, and add it. Long-press the new widget, choose Edit Widget, pick NoteSense as the script, and set When Interacting to Run Script.
  5. Copy the script again whenever you unlock a note, edit a cue or change note names, then paste it over the old one in Scriptable.
